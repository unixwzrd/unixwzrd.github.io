One of my token-optimization tests looked like a success at first. The input fell from 1,324 tokens to 1,181, and the model still returned the exact answer I expected.

Then I looked at the rest of the result. Latency had increased from 5.77 seconds to 9.14 seconds. It was one synthetic test on one dated version, but it changed the question I was asking. Had I improved the agent, or merely improved one number?

Part 5 of Local First AI and Agent Operations explains the measurement method that came out of that experience: fix or record the starting state, compare a raw baseline with exactly one candidate, retain comparable correctness evidence, record cache and warm-state effects, and make every candidate earn the failure surface it adds.

The article also looks at why a shell-output reducer performed extremely well on one noisy failure and did practically nothing useful for already concise commands.

Title:
Measuring Token Optimization Without Breaking the Agent

Subtitle:
Why fewer input tokens did not automatically produce a better result, and how I learned to measure the complete task.

Body:

One synthetic comparison reduced model input by 10.8 percent while preserving the expected answer. The same result also increased latency substantially. A separate group of requests showed almost no reduction at all.

That variation changed how I evaluate token optimization. A useful comparison needs a fixed or recorded starting state, a raw baseline, exactly one candidate path, comparable correctness evidence, and content-free measurements covering tokens, cache behavior, injected memory, tool output, latency, errors, retries, resource use, and cost where it is honestly observable.

The article applies that method to RTK, Headroom, and provider-managed prompt caching while keeping every result bound to its actual version, workload, and sample size. It also explains why bypass, rollback, removal, and a post-upgrade telemetry audit belong inside the optimization decision rather than outside it.
