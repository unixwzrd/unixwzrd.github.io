Similarity search can find the wrong thing extremely well.

Once the same agent moves among private conversations, technical work, general operations, and material still waiting for review, relevance is no longer the difficult question. The difficult question is whether this conversation, with these participants and this purpose, is allowed to receive the memory at all.

In Part 3 of my Local First AI and Agent Operations series, I explain why I now treat memory as a governance problem with a search component. LLM-Wiki inside my broader Obsidian Main Vault remains the human-readable authority; Mnemosyne is disposable derived retrieval state. I describe the coarse general/private routing boundary I use today and lay out the finer-grained policy model I am working toward.

The central rule is simple: authorization must construct the candidate set before vector or lexical ranking begins. Filtering afterward is already too late.

Title:
Memory Is a Governance Problem, Not Just a Vector Database

Subtitle:
Why relevance ranking must sit behind authority, provenance, and fail-closed access decisions.

Body:

Persistent memory becomes impressive very quickly: embed a question, retrieve nearby material, and the agent appears to remember. The harder problem arrives when one agent moves among private conversations, technical projects, general operations, and material that has not yet passed human review.

This article separates LLM-Wiki and the broader Obsidian Main Vault from disposable retrieval state, describes a practical general/private bank boundary, and explains why ordinary authorization cannot be encoded in similarity scores or an ever-growing collection of databases.

The core rule is that authorization must decide which records can become candidates before vector or lexical ranking begins. The proposed finer-grained model adds classification, compartments, audience, purpose, stable context, and policy versions without pretending that those controls are already deployed.
